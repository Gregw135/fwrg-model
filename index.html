How the model works (and what it deliberately ignores)

Existing ~613 restaurants keep their current $2.32M average volumes forever (prices rise with inflation, customer traffic flat). New restaurants open at the company's own $2.8M target. Store profit is First Watch's reported restaurant-level margin — rent already deducted. Corporate overhead starts at 10.5% of revenue (stock comp counted as a cash cost, so there's no separate share dilution) and declines 0.5pt a year to the floor you set. Renovation spending defaults to Texas Roadhouse's ~2.3% of revenue. Cash flow first pays debt down to 1x EBITDA, then holds a $50M cushion, then buys back stock at whatever multiple you set. Recession years knock 5% off traffic, 2 points off margins, and halve openings. Company store count is capped at ~2,050 (management's 2,200+ total market including franchises). Taxes: 24% of profit after renovation and interest. Interest: 6.3%. "Net income (roughly)" uses renovation spending as the stand-in for depreciation, so it runs a bit higher than GAAP would.

Things to try: move the multiple slider with buybacks on, then off — with buybacks on, the 20-year return barely cares what multiple the market pays, because a cheap stock lets the company retire more of itself. The buyback is the gyroscope. With inflation at 0, everything is in 2026 dollars and the returns shown are real (on top of inflation). Deliberately ignored: depreciation beyond renovation, franchise growth, new dayparts, any multiple re-rating, and anything going right that isn't already proven.
