How the model works (and what it deliberately ignores)

The model tracks the 586 company-owned restaurants. The 79 franchised ones contribute a flat $12.5M a year (royalties and fees ran $6.0M in the first half of 2026), no growth, no inflation — the ~5 franchise openings a year are ignored. Existing stores keep their current $2.32M average volumes (prices rise with inflation; underlying customer traffic changes by the traffic slider, default +0.5% — Investor Day (Nov 2026) showed brand awareness climbing steadily over time — less the sales-transfer slider, default 1.2%: the traffic existing stores lose each year to new restaurants opened nearby, management's own estimate (weighted average 2022–25 ~1.2%; 2026 ~1.5%, easing to ~1% by 2028). Reported traffic is the difference, −0.7% a year at the defaults, compounding across the whole fleet. New stores' $2.8M target already includes the sales they take, so transfer is a pure revenue loss for the company, never counted twice. Both affect revenue only). Store margin starts at the margin slider and drifts by the margin-change slider each year — a realistic pairing is ~0.3-0.4pts of margin lost per point of annual traffic decline, since fixed costs don't scale with guests. Openings are either a fixed count per year (the company's stated plan from 2027: ~50 company-owned; the ~55–60 figure often quoted is system-wide including franchisees) or — the default — a constant percentage of the store base (8%), so the opening pace itself compounds: 10% for 20 years would take 586 company stores to ~3,900, far past the stated ~2,200-location US market — the model stops at that cap. New restaurants open 20% below the company's $2.8M target and ramp to it by year three (half-weighted in the opening year for mid-year openings). Store profit is First Watch's reported restaurant-level margin — rent and pre-opening costs already deducted (the reported reconciliation adds back G&A and depreciation but not pre-opening), so the build-cost slider is capex only (default $2.0M, the per-store figure management told investors to use in Nov 2026). The calibration line under the headline numbers shows where that margin puts 2026 EBITDA against the company's own guidance. Corporate overhead starts at 10.0% of revenue — last quarter's actual cash overhead (reported G&A was 10.9%, of which ~0.9% was non-cash stock compensation; that part shows up instead as new shares issued each year worth ~1% of revenue — about $14M, the current run rate — at the prior year's share price, so a cheap stock dilutes more and nothing is counted twice). It declines by the points-per-year you set (default 0.3pt — 1.2pts over four years), never below a 6% floor (Chipotle runs ~5.5%, Texas Roadhouse ~3.9%, both far larger; FWRG is assumed never to get that lean). Renovation spending (maintenance plus IT) defaults to 2.5% of revenue — what management's November 2026 Investor Day method implies: about $2M of capex per new restaurant, the rest of the ~$145M capex budget is upkeep, ~$38M today and rising with the fleet (Texas Roadhouse, older and open twice the hours, runs ~2.3%). Until debt is down to your debt target — default 1× EBITDA, management's stated target (Nov 2026) — the company opens restaurants at the slider's pace and every dollar left over pays down debt; no buybacks. From the year after it gets there, the opening slider no longer applies: every spare dollar — cash after upkeep, interest and tax, plus the extra borrowing the growing EBITDA allows at the target — builds new restaurants at the build cost, so growth is self-funded and speeds up as profits grow. Stock is bought back (at whatever multiple you set) only with cash the openings can't use: in recession years, which halve openings, or once the store count reaches the market size. Interest charged is inflation plus the spread you set (default +4.5pts ≈ 6.5%, close to today's term loan at 6.59% and revolver at 7.24%), so debt costs the same in real terms regardless of the inflation slider. Inflation defaults to 2% — set it to 0 to see everything in today's dollars, with real returns. Recession years knock 5% off traffic, 2 points off margins, and halve openings. Store count is capped at management's stated ~2,200-location US market (2,121 company-owned plus the 79 franchised); once it's full, openings stop and spare cash buys back stock. Taxes: 24% of profit after interest and depreciation (6.3% of revenue, the current rate — so new builds shield income the way they do in real life; the company's unused FICA tip credits, which would cut cash taxes further, are ignored). If the settings drive the company's value below its debt, the share price floors at zero — equity holders can't lose more than everything.

Things to try: move the multiple slider with buybacks on, then off — with buybacks on, the 10-year return cares less what multiple the market pays, because a cheap stock lets the company retire more of itself. The buyback is the gyroscope. With inflation at 0, everything is in 2026 dollars and the returns shown are real (on top of inflation). Deliberately ignored: franchise growth, new dayparts, any multiple re-rating, and anything going right that isn't already proven.
